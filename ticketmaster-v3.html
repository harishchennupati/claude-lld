The problem, as the interviewer puts it

“Design the ticketing system behind a big concert on-sale, the kind Ticketmaster or BookMyShow runs. Fans open the seat map of the stadium, pick seats or ask for the best ones available, hold them for a few minutes while they pay, and get their tickets in the app. For a big show, sales open at 10:00 sharp, and about a million fans are on the site for 50,000 tickets: 40,000 numbered seats and a standing pit of 10,000. A seat must never be sold to two fans, and we must never take a fan’s money for tickets we can’t give her. The map can be a few seconds behind. The sale must be fair, bots must not grab the tickets, and every other show on the site must keep selling.”

What this really is. A ticket on-sale is 50,000 tiny locks, one per ticket, sold in a few minutes to a crowd twenty times larger. Almost everyone in the crowd will leave with nothing, so the crowd must cost us almost nothing, and only as many fans as the tickets can serve are let in to buy. In scope: one big show’s on-sale: the line, the seat map, holds on seats, best available and the pit, payment through a provider, tickets in the app, and where it all runs. Out of scope: searching and browsing events, prices and fees, cancellations, refunds and exchanges after purchase, resale and transfers, scanning tickets at the gate, paying the promoter.

Start

The fans, the tickets, and what is asked

Who is who, and one fan's two tickets from the line to her phone

Three roles, in the order a request travels. The design and every picture use the role names; the example next to each is only so the story has faces.

  1. The fan, who buys tickets from her phone or browser. Example: Asha, fan f_42, who wants two seats for a Coldplay show.
  2. Us, the ticketing platform. We run the waiting line, the seat map, holds, orders and tickets, and we never see her card. Example: BookMyShow.
  3. The payment provider, which takes her payment on its own page, by card with the OTP her bank sends her, or by UPI, and tells us the result. Example: Razorpay.

A ticket is the right to one place: a numbered seat, or one of the 10,000 standing places in the pit, the area in front of the stage. The edge means the CDN's servers in many cities (a CDN is a network of caching servers close to the users) and our small functions on them. The line is our virtual waiting room at the edge. The door is the point in the line up to which fans are let in; it moves forward at a rate we set, and a fan who has been let in is inside.

Many eyes, one owner. The million fans only watch, and the edge serves what they watch. Each ticket's owner is decided by one guarded write in the database that holds the event's tickets: a statement that takes the ticket only if, at that very moment, it is still free. Every mechanism on this page keeps the watching cheap and the owning exact.

Waiting. Only accounts that registered for the on-sale, each with a verified phone number, may join its line, and registration closes a week ahead. A fan joins the line and gets a line token: a record, kept in her browser, of her account, the event and when she joined, signed so that she cannot change it. At 10:00:00 the draw gives every fan who joined before 10:00 a random number. The line is ordered by those numbers, lowest first, and the door starts moving up through them. A fan let in gets two things: a pass, which is a signed permission to hold seats for this event, and one admissions row in the database, which records that she was let in.

Inside. She looks at the seat map, one small file served by the edge and a few seconds old at most. A hold claims her tickets for 8 minutes. If she has not tapped Pay by then, the hold lapses: nothing is written, and its seats simply count as free once its time has passed. The 8 minutes only have to last until she taps Pay, because paying gets its own 5 minutes. Pit places are stored like seats, so every rule about a hold's seats covers them too.

Paying. Tapping Pay moves the hold to PAYING, which keeps its seats until a deadline, pay_by, 5 minutes after the tap. It also creates her order, which stays PENDING until the sale. Her bank authorises: it approves the payment and sets the money aside, blocked on her card or already taken from her UPI account. A hold is always our hold on tickets, never money her bank sets aside. The sale, one transaction, makes the tickets hers. Then we capture: we tell the provider to keep the money. The diagram follows her two seats for event 912, 114-R12-S7 and 114-R12-S8 (section 114, row 12), shortened to S7 and S8.

If she lets us choose. If she asks for "the best 2" or for 2 places in the pit, the same flow runs, and the booking service picks the seats for her.

What the interviewer expects: five jobs, and a crowd twenty times the tickets

  1. Let a million fans wait for 10:00 and let them in, in a fair order, at a pace the tickets and our servers can serve.
  2. Show each fan inside a seat map that is at most a few seconds behind.
  3. Hold the seats she picks, the best available ones, or places in the pit, for 8 minutes, never giving one ticket to two fans.
  4. Take her payment and turn the hold into tickets, never keeping her money for tickets we cannot give.
  5. Stop bots, and one person with many accounts, from taking the tickets: at most six per fan.

It must survive the jump at 10:00:00, when 800,000 waiting pages, which only count down until then, all ask for their place within seconds. It must survive the event's database or a whole data centre dying mid-sale, a payment provider that is slow, failing or down, and bots with thousands of accounts. It must never give one ticket to two fans, because no apology fixes two fans at one seat. A position, a wait or a map a few seconds old is fine, because a tap on a seat that is no longer free costs one refused request. Inside the event's home region, the one city where its database runs, no single failure of ours may stop holds for more than about 30 seconds. No such failure may lose a sale. A provider outage may pause payment, and nothing else. And every other event on the site must keep selling as if the big show were not happening.

The two numbers. The assumptions: an order has 2.5 tickets on average. Of the fans who are let in, 60% buy. Each fan inside tries about 3 holds and stays about 180 seconds.

Each stays about 180 s, so by Little's law (fans inside = arrivals a second × seconds each stays) the door moves at 20,000 ÷ 180 ≈ 110 fans a second. The rest follows by proportion. Tickets sell at 66 orders × 2.5 = 165 a second, and at that rate the stock would last about five minutes. The sell-out comes later, about 8 to 10 minutes after 10:00, for two reasons. Sales reach that rate only about three minutes after 10:00, when the first fans let in have finished choosing. And tickets in abandoned holds come back only when those holds lapse.

The 50,000 tickets make 20,000 orders, and only 60% of the fans let in buy, so only about 20,000 ÷ 0.6 ≈ 33,000 fans are ever let in. The other 967,000 learn "sold out" from the same cached status document. We expect 800,000 of the million to join before 10:00, and 200,000 more in the minute after. At 10:00:00 each of those 800,000 pages asks at a random moment in the next 10 seconds: 80,000 requests a second at the edge. Inside, 20,000 fans fetch the 10 KB map every 2 seconds: 10,000 CDN requests a second, about 100 MB a second.

Everything stored is small, a few hundred thousand rows, so the hard part is contention and the crowd, never size.

A waiting fan only polls the edge, and no connection stays open. A fan inside makes a few REST calls, each answered while she waits.

If the interviewer pushes

  • Can a new release change the line token's format while fans are waiting? Not during the on-sale: deploys are frozen from an hour before. A format change ships days earlier, and the edge reads both formats until every old token has expired.
  • What does the app do when a call fails? 503 means our servers for this event are busy or failing over: wait for the Retry-After header plus a random 1 to 5 seconds, so 20,000 apps do not come back in the same instant. 429 means she has a hold in flight, or is sending holds too fast: wait a few seconds. 409 means a seat was taken: offer the nearby seats, never re-send the same seats. 410 means the hold is gone: pick seats again. 401 means her pass ended: rejoin the line at the back.

How to derive this design yourselfthe method

An on-sale asks two things of every choice: does it keep a million-strong crowd off the database, and does it give each ticket exactly one owner? The functional rows take the jobs in the order they start to matter: each starts from what an ordinary engineer tries first, finds the way it breaks at 10:00, and fixes it. The non-functional rows then add what that design lacks to survive another event's crowd, a failing provider, a dying primary, and the peak load. Each fix names the boxes it adds, and the arrow after it names the section that shows how it works. Together, the twelve rows force every box of the design picture.

F1. Let the crowd wait for 10:00, then let fans in at a pace we can servePut the site behind autoscaling and let everyone load the seat map at 10:00.The crowd arrives within a minute, but autoscaling needs minutes, and every map load reads the database that must also sell the seats. The site falls over before a seat is sold.A line at the edge that writes no row: each fan carries a signed line token. The admission controller moves the door and writes a status document to object storage, from which the edge answers every poll; the admission service swaps her token for a pass. A line, not a rate limiter at the load balancer. A rate limiter is one setting and protects the servers just as well. But it lets in whoever retries fastest, which is a script, and every refused fan reloads. A line lets in the same 110 a second in a fixed order, and tells each fan where she stands.
Our own line, not a bought waiting room. A bought room runs today, with its own bot defence and its own draw, and our controller could steer its rate through its API. But its line lives with one vendor: if that vendor fails at 10:00, the line is gone, while our numbers are a keyed hash that a second CDN recomputes. Ours also checks its pass inside the hold transaction, so a pass that has ended never slips a hold in.
Signed tokens at the edge, not a queue table or a Redis list of waiting fans. A table gives an exact line that can be queried, but it costs an insert per fan in the busiest minute and a lookup on every poll. A token costs one signing key; its price is an approximate line, in which a fan who gives up is invisible.
Polling the edge every 10 seconds, not a WebSocket or a long poll per waiting fan. A socket, or a request held open until there is news, would tell her the instant her turn comes. But a million of them need a fleet of connection servers, and one of them dying starts a reconnect storm: all its fans reconnect at the same moment. A wait of minutes can take its news every 10 seconds, from the edge's cache.

A file polled through the CDN, not changes pushed over SSE or WebSocket. SSE (server-sent events, a one-way stream from a server to the browser) would show a change in about 200 ms instead of about 2 seconds. But each of the 20,000 fans inside would keep a connection open to a server of ours, while the CDN serves one cached file to all of them. And a stale seat costs only one 409, answered in milliseconds.
One file for the whole event, not tiles per section. Tiles would fetch only the section she looks at. But the whole map is 10 KB, smaller than one photo on the event page, and one file is one build and one version, so every fan sees the same moment.
F3. Hold the seats a fan picks, never giving a seat to two fansRead the seats' status, and if they are available, write HELD.Two fans tap the same seat in the same millisecond. Both reads see AVAILABLE, both write HELD, and both fans go on to pay for one seat.The claim: one guarded UPDATE over all the hold's seats, all or nothing, in the booking service's transaction on the event database. (→ Two fans, one seat)
A guarded UPDATE, not SELECT … FOR UPDATE kept while she pays. Locking the rows first and deciding afterwards is the simplest way to be sure. But a row lock lasts until its transaction commits, so 20,000 fans inside would each keep a transaction and a database connection open for up to 8 minutes.
A guarded UPDATE answered while she waits, not a queue of hold requests. A queue with one consumer per event removes every race and absorbs a burst. But each hold becomes asynchronous, so the app must poll for an answer it wanted in 50 ms, and the losers only wait longer. The line is already the queue in front of the holds.
Postgres, not Redis or DynamoDB. Both can claim one seat safely and fast. But a hold is one decision over several rows: every seat free, and her total within six. Redis can make it in a script, but only if all its keys share one hash slot, the unit that Redis Cluster places on one node. And because Redis answers before its replica has the write, a failover can lose holds that fans were told they have. DynamoDB needs a transaction over several items, at twice the write cost. One Postgres primary makes the decision in one ordinary transaction, and it is big enough (N4).
F4. Take back seats a fan abandonsA job that runs every minute and frees holds whose time has run out.Seats wait up to a minute after their hold ends, in the busiest minutes of the on-sale. And if the job stops, the show looks sold out while seats sit empty.A hold has an expiry, held_until, written on each seat row, and the claim of F3 also takes seats whose expiry has passed. A seat can be taken the moment its hold lapses, whether or not anything has cleaned up. (→ Two fans, one seat)
Expiry checked by the claim, not a cleaning job or a Redis TTL. A cleaning job keeps the rows tidy, and a Redis TTL (time to live) deletes a key by itself. But when Redis deletes an expired key, it sends one notice, with no retry. A job that frees the seat in the database on that notice can miss it, and the seat stays held. The price of checking in the claim: a row keeps saying HELD after its time, so every reader must compare held_until with the clock.
F5. Sell "the best 4 together" and places in the standing pitGive each request the best free block of seats; for the pit, the first free places by id.Requests in the same half-second compute the same answer: dozens collide on the same four seats, or wait on the same pit rows, and the losers collide again on the next best.For seats, a weighted random pick among the best 1,000 blocks (groups of adjacent free seats of the size asked) in the booking service's copy of the map, then the claim of F3. For the pit, a row per pit place, claimed with FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED, so claims at the same moment take different rows. (→ When we choose the seats)
A row per pit place, not a counter. A counter (free = free − 2) is one row and very simple. But every pit hold would queue on its lock, and when a hold lapses nothing gives its places back, so a cleaning job would be needed again, with F4's problem: places wait for it, and if it stops, the pit looks sold out. Rows lapse exactly like seats, for the cost of 10,000 small rows.
F6. Take payment, and never keep money for tickets we cannot giveSend her to the payment provider, and when it says approved, mark the seats sold.Her bank's OTP page takes most of a minute, so her hold can lapse mid-payment, and the approval then arrives for a seat another fan holds.PAYING first, provider second: one transaction of ours moves the hold to PAYING, keeping its seats, and creates the order; only then do we create a payment request at the payment provider. A payment checker, a worker, asks the provider about every payment whose answer never came. (→ Paying before the clock runs out)
Authorise, sell, then capture; not charge in one step. Charging in one step is one call instead of two, but we would own her money before we know there are seats, and every lost sale would need a refund that we must start and track. The provider waits a set time for our capture, the capture window; the money of an approval we never capture starts going back to her when it ends, even if we have crashed for good.
Five minutes to pay, not the provider's own 10. The provider waits up to 10 minutes for a bank that does not answer, and can learn of an approval for up to 3 days, though such late approvals are under 0.5% of payments. Keeping seats that long would freeze them for most of an on-sale that lasts minutes. So we keep them 5 minutes, and a later approval still sells while her hold is PAYING and no one has taken her seats.
F7. A fair order at 10:00, with no advantage for scriptsFirst come, first served.A script sends its request at 10:00:00.001 and wins every first-come race, and so does anyone with a faster connection.The draw: everyone who joins before 10:00 gets a random number at 10:00, and later arrivals queue by join time. Only accounts registered with the account service may join, and each gets one number. The edge gives each request a bot score, how likely it is that a script sent it; a request with a high score must first pass a challenge, a test easy for a person and hard for a script. (→ The line; Bots and one person with many accounts)
A draw at 10:00, not first come, first served. First come feels fairer between people, but only the draw makes arriving early worth nothing to a script. Its cost is that a fan who waited since 09:30 can end up behind one who came at 09:59.
F8. At most six tickets per fanCheck the fan's ticket count in the API, before the hold.Forty holds sent at once from one account all pass the check, because none of them can see the others yet.Every hold first locks the fan's own admissions row, so her holds run one at a time. It counts her tickets under that lock. (→ Bots and one person with many accounts)
Count her tickets under her row's lock, not a counter column. A counter column is one read. But whenever a PAYING hold ends without a sale, its tickets must come off the counter, one more write that can be missed. Adding up her holds under the lock is right every time.

Part 2

The seven hard parts

The line: a million fans, and a door that lets in 110 a second

The problem. At 09:59:59, 800,000 browsers are open on the waiting page. If the booking service simply opened at 10:00:00, 800,000 requests would reach it within seconds, for tickets that 20,000 fans can buy.

The fix is a virtual waiting room at the edge: fans who join before the on-sale opens form a pre-queue, and the door moves along it at a rate we set, which is called admission control:

  1. At 10:00:00, the draw fixes a number for every fan in the pre-queue: HMAC(draw key, fan id, event id), a hash that can be computed only with a secret key, the draw key. The hash is read as a fraction between 0 and 1; fan f_42's is 0.0110. It needs no row, is the same on each of her devices that joined before 10:00, and cannot be known early, because only the edge and the admission service know the draw key.
  2. The event has one row in the doors table, whose column draw_upto says how far the door has reached: every fan whose number is at or below it has had her turn. The 800,000 numbers are spread evenly between 0 and 1, so to let in 110 fans a second, the admission controller adds 110 ÷ 800,000 = 0.0001375 to draw_upto every second, to start with. The 800,000, counted once per account from the edge's log of joins, need not be exact, because the door's speed is corrected from real admissions.
  3. The edge works out her position from the status document: (her number − draw_upto) × 800,000.
  4. Her turn stays open for 10 minutes. For this, the doors row also keeps draw_from, where the door stood 10 minutes ago, read from its column marks. A fan may come in while her number is above draw_from and at or below draw_upto. The edge forwards her /admit only once her turn has come. The admission service then writes her admissions row and signs her pass, which gives her a separate 10 minutes to hold seats, counted from when she comes in (pass_until). Fan f_42 comes in the moment her turn opens, so both happen to end at 10:11:20.

A position needs no row: it is the distance from her number to the door.

-- the admission service, on her POST /admit (her number: 0.0110)
INSERT INTO admissions (event_id, fan_id, pass_id, admitted_at, pass_until)
SELECT 912, 'f_42', 'ps_4410', now(), now() + interval '10 minutes' FROM doors
WHERE event_id = 912 AND state <> 'paused'
  AND draw_from < 0.0110 AND 0.0110 <= draw_upto;
-- 1 row: she is in; sign pass ps_4410; her pass_until is 10:11:20
-- 0 rows: not her turn, her turn is over, or the on-sale was stopped by hand

INSERT … SELECT inserts a row only if the SELECT finds one, so her row appears only while her turn is open. A fan who joined after 10:00 has no number, so she is let in by her join time instead: join_from and join_upto work for join times exactly as draw_from and draw_upto do for numbers.

If the interviewer pushes

  • She misses her 10-minute turn. Can she come back? Yes, but only at the back of the line, by her new join time, so for a show this size the tickets are gone first. She has no admissions row yet, so she would get one when that turn came.
  • Her pass ends before she buys. Can she come back? Yes, the same way, but her admissions row already exists. So the INSERT carries ON CONFLICT … DO UPDATE, which updates that row instead, only once its pass_until has passed, so nobody holds two passes at once.

Moving the door: one controller at a time, and three brakes

The problem. At 10:02:30 a third of the numbers the door passes belong to closed tabs, so only about 73 fans a second come in instead of 110, and tickets sit unsold. A rate fixed in advance cannot see this, and the controller that moves the door can itself stall.

The fix is the loop at the bottom of the line's picture: every second the admission controller moves the door, and three brakes adjust its rate.

-- the admission controller, every second, while it has the lease
UPDATE doors SET draw_upto = draw_upto + 0.0001375
WHERE event_id = 912 AND epoch = 8 AND lease_until > now();
-- 0 rows: its lease ran out, or epoch 9 has taken over: it stops

Only one controller may move the door. It renews a lease on the doors row every 3 seconds, setting lease_until 10 seconds ahead. A spare takes over only once lease_until has passed, and raises the row's epoch by one. Every UPDATE names its epoch, so an old controller that wakes after a takeover changes 0 rows: the epoch is a fencing token, checked where the data is written. The status document carries the epoch and its time, so the edge ignores an older one.

The seat map: one 10 KB file, about two seconds behind

The problem. At 10:03, 20,000 fans are inside, each refreshing the map every 2 seconds. If each view asked the event's database for its section's 500 seats, that would be 10,000 queries a second and 5 million seat rows read a second, on the one primary that must also take 330 holds a second. And about 1,500 seat rows change every second, so any copy of them is stale within a second.

The fix is a projection, a copy of the truth shaped for reading, which may lag. Every 500 ms, the map builder reads all 50,000 event_seats rows of event 912 from the replica. It packs the 40,000 seats at 2 bits each, and the pit's free, held and sold counts, into one binary map file of 10 KB, drawn in the picture's inset.

The rule that keeps it harmless. Nothing durable is written, and the map never decides anything; the claim does. By the rule in the inset, a seat whose hold has lapsed shows as free at the next build, though nothing has written to it. The booking service keeps its own copy of the file in memory, for best available and for the nearby seats of a 409, and that copy decides nothing either.

The CDN caches the file in two layers. The first is its edge locations: its servers in each city. The second is its origin shield: a middle cache in front of object storage. Each edge location keeps the file for only 1 second, so without the shield, every edge location would ask object storage for it once a second. The shield collects those requests, so object storage sees one a second.

The one hole. Seats freed by lapsed holds appear on 20,000 maps at the same moment, and hundreds of fans tap the same few. One wins each seat, and the rest get a 409 with nearby seats to try instead.

If the interviewer pushes

  • A fan on a slow mobile network: is 10 KB every 2 seconds too much? It is 40 kbit/s, a fraction of the lowest-quality video stream, and it compresses well, because whole rows share one state. On a slow connection the app polls every 5 seconds.
  • What if the replica falls behind? The header carries read_at and the replica's lag, so the app shows the map's age once it is more than 8 seconds old; the watch table in "How it grows" pages someone sooner. Holds are not affected, because the claim reads the primary.

Two fans, one seat: the same millisecond, one guarded UPDATE

The problem. At 10:03:10.412 Asha asks for 114-R12-S7 and S8, and in the same millisecond fan 58 asks for S8 and S9. The map both of them see is a second old and shows all three seats free. Only one of them can have S8.

The fix is the claim: one guarded UPDATE for all the seats of the hold. Its WHERE clause states the rule, "each seat is AVAILABLE, or HELD by a hold whose time has passed".

There are three ways to stop this race. Pessimistic locking locks the rows first and then decides. Optimistic locking is a write that succeeds only if the row still says what we saw. A unique constraint lets the database refuse a second row for the same seat. The claim is a compare-and-set, close to the optimistic kind: the database checks its WHERE on each row at the moment it writes that row.

The claim is safe at READ COMMITTED, Postgres's default isolation level (the technical round weighs SERIALIZABLE).

Only the database sees both requests at once, so only it can pick the winner.

INSERT INTO holds (id, event_id, fan_id, status, n_tickets, expires_at)
VALUES ('h_77', 912, 'f_42', 'HELD', 2, now() + interval '8 minutes');
UPDATE event_seats
SET status = 'HELD', hold_id = 'h_77', held_until = now() + interval '8 minutes'
WHERE event_id = 912
  AND seat_id IN ('114-R12-S7', '114-R12-S8')
  AND (status = 'AVAILABLE' OR (status = 'HELD' AND held_until < now()));
-- 2 rows changed: both seats are hers: COMMIT, 201
-- fewer than 2: ROLLBACK, which removes the hold row too; 409 naming the taken seats

now() is the time the transaction started, so both statements write the same expiry, 10:11:10.412. The whole hold is one transaction in three steps. First, it locks her admissions row, releases her unpaid hold and counts her tickets (see "Bots and one person with many accounts"). Second, it inserts the hold row, because each seat's hold_id must name a hold that exists. Third, it runs the claim.

Within one statement, a claim locks the seats in id order, through the primary-key index. So two claims that share seats never end up each waiting for the other, which is called a deadlock. Other writes that touch seats, such as freeing her old hold or a sale, can still lock them in another order. When a deadlock happens, Postgres notices it within one second (its deadlock_timeout setting), cancels one of the two transactions, and we retry it.

The one hole. Everything that sells a seat must go through this guard. A bug, or a fix typed by hand at a database prompt, could still write SOLD twice for one seat, so the last net is a unique index on tickets (event_id, seat_id), which refuses a second ticket for one seat.

If the interviewer pushes

  • A thousand fans tap the same freed front-row seat within a second. What does the queue on that one row cost? Very little. The winner keeps the row's lock for a few milliseconds, until its commit, and every other claim then re-checks and rolls back with a 409. The losers cost the database only a connection each while they wait, and the 409's nearby seats spread their next taps out.
  • Why not a reservation row per seat under a unique index, the constraint way? It works: an insert with ON CONFLICT … DO UPDATE … WHERE held_until < now() takes over a lapsed reservation in one statement. But the seat row already exists for the map and the sale, so the guarded UPDATE is the same rule without a second table.

When we choose the seats: best available and the pit, without collisions

The problem. At 10:03:40 about 55 fans in the same half-second ask for "the best 4 together" in the gold tier, and every request reads the same map and scores it the same way.

The fix is a weighted random pick. A block is a group of adjacent free seats in one row, of exactly the size asked; each row's free seats are cut into blocks side by side, so no two blocks share a seat. The booking service scores the tier's blocks in its copy of the map, by closeness to the stage and the centre and by not leaving one seat alone between taken ones. It picks one of the best 1,000 at random, where a better block is a little more likely to be picked: the best is three times as likely as the 1,000th. Then it claims the block with the guarded UPDATE of "Two fans, one seat". On a 409 it picks again, up to 2 more times. Each new pick leaves out the seats this booking server has claimed, or heard are taken, since its copy of the map was built.

Spreading the fans over many good blocks makes most first tries win.

Where the numbers come from. About 110 best-available requests arrive a second, a third of all holds. In the worst case all of them want the gold tier, so about 55 share each half-second version of the map.

FOR UPDATE locks the rows the SELECT returns until the transaction ends, so the UPDATE that follows cannot find them changed. SKIP LOCKED makes the SELECT pass over rows another claim has locked, instead of waiting for them.

The one hole. Near the end, SKIP LOCKED also passes over places locked by claims that are about to roll back, so a fan can hear "none left" a moment before two places come free. The app tries once more after a second, and the map's pit counts show how many places are held, not only sold.

If the interviewer pushes

  • Why doesn't the app simply pick again from its map after a 409? Its map would give every loser the same second choice, and each retry costs a round trip over a phone network. The server picks again in milliseconds, and already knows which seats were just taken.
  • Couldn't one allocator per section hand out blocks without any collisions? It would never offer a block twice, but it is a single process per section that must be restarted after a crash, with a guard so that an old copy and a new one never both hand out blocks. The pick needs no coordination; a collision costs one more statement.

Bots and one person with many accounts: one unpaid hold at a time, and a limit of six

The problem. At 10:00:00 a scalper, someone who buys tickets to resell them at a higher price, runs 3,000 scripted browsers on 3,000 accounts, and each script fires 40 hold requests at once the moment it is let in.

The fix is layers that each make a bot pay more, and one exact guard: every hold first locks her own admissions row, so her holds run one at a time. Under that lock, a new hold releases her unpaid hold and counts the tickets she is paying for or has bought.

Her own row is the one place that every hold of hers must pass.

FOR UPDATE NOWAIT locks her row until the transaction ends, and fails at once if another of her holds has it, so holds of hers that arrive together cost one hold and a quick 429 each, not a connection each waiting on her row. RETURNING hands back the ids of the holds the first UPDATE released, and ANY(:released) lets the seat UPDATE free only their seats, so seats another fan took after her hold lapsed are left alone. coalesce turns "no rows" into 0. If the claim that follows fails, the whole transaction rolls back, and she keeps the hold she had.

The lock is on her own row, so two different fans never wait for each other. A PAYING hold is never replaced, because her money may already be approved for its seats. So she may hold new seats while her bank's page is open, and the PAYING hold's tickets count towards her six.

The release writes the hold's row first and its seats second, the order that checkout uses in the next section, so a release and a checkout of the same hold never deadlock on its seats. If a checkout in another tab has just made her hold PAYING, the release waits for the hold's row, re-checks status = 'HELD', finds PAYING and frees nothing.

The one hole. Bot defence is a cost, not a wall: people paid to queue with real accounts still get through.

If the interviewer pushes

  • One person with 50 verified accounts: what stops them? Some providers return a fingerprint of the card: an id that is the same every time that card is used, but is not the card number. We store it on the order, as payer_fingerprint, and when orders for this event that share one payer add up to more than six tickets, a person is asked to check them. It narrows the trick; it does not close it.
  • Bots take holds and never pay, to keep seats off sale. What bounds that? Each account that gets in freezes at most six tickets. It can keep them off sale for its pass's 10 minutes, plus 8 minutes for the last hold it makes, plus up to 5 minutes if that hold goes to PAYING: about 23 minutes at most. Replacing holds freezes nothing more. The draw lets in about 33,000 of the 800,000, about 4% of any group, so a scalper's 3,000 accounts get about 120 passes: at most about 720 tickets frozen, 1.4% of the stock.

Paying before the clock runs out: PAYING first, then the provider

The problem. Suppose Asha taps Pay on hold h_77 at 10:10:50, twenty seconds before it lapses at 10:11:10, and her bank's OTP page takes 41 seconds. Her hold would lapse while she types, and her seats could go to another fan before her money is approved.

The fix is PAYING first, provider second: our own commit comes before any call to the provider. The provider's page is set to close at pay_by, but a page already open in her browser may still let her pay after that. Such a late payment meets the approval rule below. A failed attempt, such as a mistyped OTP, changes nothing: she can try again until the page closes.

What we do first, commit PAYING or call the provider, decides whether her seats wait for her.

-- 10:10:50, before any call to the provider
UPDATE holds SET status = 'PAYING', pay_by = now() + interval '5 minutes'
WHERE id = 'h_77' AND fan_id = 'f_42' AND status = 'HELD' AND expires_at > now();
-- 0 rows: lapsed (410), or already PAYING (a retry: return the same order)
UPDATE event_seats SET held_until = now() + interval '5 minutes'
WHERE event_id = 912 AND hold_id = 'h_77' AND status = 'HELD';
-- seats stay HELD (a seat is AVAILABLE, HELD or SOLD); only held_until moves
-- must be 2 rows (a claim may have taken a seat as the hold lapsed); fewer: ROLLBACK, 410
INSERT INTO orders (id, event_id, fan_id, hold_id, total, status)
VALUES ('o_31', 912, 'f_42', 'h_77', 9000, 'PENDING');
COMMIT;

The approval rule. An approval can reach us by the provider's webhook, by her return page, or through the payment checker below, so the same approval can arrive more than once. We capture a payment only when its sale has committed. The sale commits only while the hold is PAYING, every seat still carries the hold's id, and the amount the provider approved is the order's total. Any other approval is never captured, and the provider starts returning its money when the capture window ends. We set that window to one day, longer than any delay in our own capture, such as a provider outage.

-- the sale, when approval pp_8812 for 912-o_31 arrives, for 9000
SELECT status FROM holds WHERE id = 'h_77' FOR UPDATE;   -- one approval at a time
-- o_31 already CONFIRMED with pp_8812: a repeat; write nothing,
--   and leave any capture to the checker
UPDATE event_seats SET status = 'SOLD', order_id = 'o_31', held_until = NULL
WHERE event_id = 912 AND hold_id = 'h_77' AND status = 'HELD';   -- must be 2 rows
UPDATE holds SET status = 'SOLD' WHERE id = 'h_77' AND status = 'PAYING';
INSERT INTO tickets (id, event_id, seat_id, order_id) VALUES
  ('t_311', 912, '114-R12-S7', 'o_31'), ('t_312', 912, '114-R12-S8', 'o_31');
UPDATE orders SET status = 'CONFIRMED', provider_payment = 'pp_8812'
WHERE id = 'o_31' AND status = 'PENDING';
COMMIT;   -- only now: capture pp_8812, then stamp captured_at

FOR UPDATE on the hold row makes a second approval wait, and then see what the first one did. If the seat UPDATE changes fewer than 2 rows, because a seat was claimed after pay_by, the sale rolls back and the approval is never captured. A second transaction then releases the hold: the hold becomes RELEASED, the order EXPIRED, and any seat still carrying the hold AVAILABLE again.

The payment checker never decides a payment by itself: it asks the provider, and the approval rule decides.

By the first ask, our call to create a request has long given up, at its 5-second timeout. A checkout retry can race that release, and whichever commits first wins: the release needs the order still without a request id, and the retry can store one only while the order is PENDING.

The capture waits for the replica because of how Postgres commits. A commit waiting for its copy is already on the primary's disk, but hidden from other sessions until the wait ends. If that wait is cancelled, or the primary restarts, the sale becomes visible on the primary alone, and a failover then loses it. For the same reason, the booking service never cancels that wait.

The one hole. A payment still unknown at pay_by can lose its seats to another fan, and she is then told the tickets are gone. A capture the provider refuses, because her bank reversed the approval or our capture missed the capture window, goes to a person, like any cancellation after purchase.

If the interviewer pushes

  • Her approval arrives after her seats have gone. How soon does she get her money back? Only after the one-day capture window, because our provider cannot refund a payment we never captured. The money then takes 5 to 7 working days to reach her. A business that finds that too long can capture such a payment and refund it at once, for a fee.
  • She opens two tabs and pays in both. Is she charged twice? If both attempts are approved, the hold's row lock makes them take turns: the first sells and is captured. The second finds the order CONFIRMED with another payment, so it is never captured, and its money goes back to her after the one-day window.

Running it

Where it runs: the edge, one home region per event, and a cell for the big show

A region is one city where the cloud provider has data centres, with three availability zones in it (N3 defines a zone). The event database, which decides every owner, runs in one region, together with the servers that write to it. That is because every ticket must have one owner decided in one place, and selling the same seats from two regions would need a round trip between them on every hold. Each event gets one home region, the one nearest its venue: Mumbai for this show.

Everything about the on-sale is known a week ahead, so it is prepared, not autoscaled.

If the interviewer pushes

  • The load test finds the primary's ceiling at 3,000 write transactions a second, not 5,000. What changes before 10:00? Nothing for this show: 700 is still under a quarter of 3,000, and the door, not the database, sets the rate. Only the point at which an event must split its sections over several primaries moves down, to about 3,000.
  • An event in the shared cell turns out to be hot only on the day. What protects the others? Its own door. The door's rate is set per event, from its stock, so a surprise crowd waits in that event's line at the edge instead of reaching the shared database.

Seats refuse, the crowd keeps watching: the choice during a network split

Sometimes the network splits, and two groups of machines cannot reach each other: a network partition. During one, each part of the system must choose between consistency, where every read sees the latest write, and availability, where every request gets an answer, perhaps a stale one. This system makes that CAP choice part by part, by what a wrong answer would cost.

The same parts choose again when the network is fine, this time between speed and consistency. The line and the map choose speed: the edge answers from a copy a second or two old. If the interviewer pushes

  • Fans far from Mumbai, say in Europe: what do they feel? The line and the map come from the edge near them. Only a hold or a checkout crosses to the region, about 150 ms more, which a fan tapping a seat does not notice.
  • The link from the edge to Mumbai drops for a minute while fans' turns come. Does the real-admissions brake read that as closed tabs, and move the door ahead too fast? No. Closed tabs lower admissions a little at a time, but a split cuts them below a quarter of the rate at once, which the brake treats as a fault, so the door stays still. Fans whose turn came during that minute can still come in for the rest of their 10-minute turn.

When something breaks during the on-sale: undersell, never oversell

An on-sale is judged by Start's 30-second promise, not by a yearly downtime figure. In the table below, "data lost" is the RPO (recovery point objective: how much data a failure loses), and "back in" is the RTO (recovery time objective: how long until we are back).

A primary that cannot renew its lease from the cluster manager (N3) stops writing. Once that lease has run out, the cluster manager makes both copies disconnect from the old primary, and only then promotes one. So a primary that was only frozen, and wakes after that, finds no copy to confirm its commits, and two primaries never commit at once; this is called fencing.

The failure that matters most is the event database's primary, because every hold and sale waits on it.

A bad UPDATE typed at a database prompt reaches the copies in a millisecond, so they are no defence against it. It is fixed forward, by new writes: the tickets rows, the confirmed orders and each sold seat's order_id all record the sales, so sold seats are set back to SOLD and the rest to AVAILABLE. Only holds are lost: their fans, even those paying, must pick again, and no seat is sold twice. If the mistake reached those records too, each cell's nightly base backup, replayed with its archived write-ahead log (Postgres's record of every change), restores a copy of the database to the second before the mistake. Only the damaged rows are copied back from it, because restoring in place would throw away every sale made since.

If the interviewer pushes

  • Her pay_by passes during the 30 seconds, while her bank is approving. Does she lose her seats? Not if her payment is approved in time: the extension drawn above gives her the 30 seconds back. The provider re-sends a refused webhook only after a growing delay, which may come too late, so it is the checker's round at 10:04:50 that finds her approval while every seat still carries her hold.
  • The CDN running the line fails in some cities at 10:00:05. Do the fans there lose their place? No. Her number is a keyed hash of her account, not a row. The second CDN has the same line code and keys, and reads the same status document from object storage, so it gives her the same number and position. DNS moves most fans there within a minute or two, and their browsers send the same line token.

The technical round: eight questions interviewers push onthe last round
Why is the hold's expiry copied onto every seat row, instead of the claim joining the holds table?

The claim must decide on the row it is locking. A join would read the hold row without locking it. Suppose one fan's checkout is moving her hold to PAYING and setting her seats' new expiry. At that moment another fan's claim could read the old expiry from the hold row and take the seat: the gap between a read and a write would be back. With held_until on the seat row itself, a claim that waited for the seat's lock re-checks the new value. The same transactions write both copies, so they never drift apart.

Why is a hold 8 minutes, and what does each extra minute cost?

It only has to last until she taps Pay (Start, "Inside"). We assume nearly every fan who buys taps Pay within 6 minutes of holding, and 8 covers them with room to spare. A longer hold costs stock. Of the 110 fans let in each second, 40% leave without buying: about 44 a second. If half of them leave a hold of 2.5 tickets behind, that is 22 × 2.5 = 55 tickets a second going into abandoned holds. So each extra minute of hold keeps 55 × 60 = 3,300 tickets off sale at the peak, which is why the biggest on-sales get the shortest timers. The watch table checks the 6-minute assumption.

Would you run the hold at SERIALIZABLE instead, and let Postgres catch the race?

It would be correct. SERIALIZABLE, Postgres's strictest isolation level, lets a plain read-then-write run and aborts one of two transactions whose reads and writes cross. But the loser learns it only once it has done its work, at a write or at commit, and must retry the whole hold, with its release and its count. And the pit's claim scans a range of places, so it can abort pit holds that never shared a place with it. The guarded UPDATE gets the same safety at READ COMMITTED: its WHERE is checked again on the row it has locked, so only fans who want the same seat wait for each other, and a loser learns within milliseconds which seats were taken.

At fifty times this rate, where would Redis help, and what would stay in Postgres?

Redis would help as a filter in front of the database. Each claim first sets one Redis key per seat with SET NX, which succeeds only if the key does not exist yet, and each key lives about 10 seconds. This rejects most losing claims in a tenth of a millisecond. A claim that then rolls back in Postgres tries to delete its keys, but nothing makes sure it does; a missed delete costs seconds of wrong 409s on a free seat, not 8 minutes. The guarded UPDATE stays the truth. Redis can lose keys in a failover, and then some collisions reach Postgres, which still refuses them. Redis decides how much work the database sees, never who owns a seat.

The door's rate comes from a 180-second guess. What happens when the guess is wrong?

If fans really take 360 seconds, twice the guess, the inside grows to 40,000: more 409s, and the stock brake pauses the door sooner, because every ticket is held. So the controller measures the real time inside, from admitted_at to the order or to the end of the fan's hold, and re-derives the rate from Little's law every minute: 20,000 ÷ 360 s is about 55 a second. If the guess is too long, the opposite happens: fewer than 20,000 fans are inside, so tickets sell more slowly than they could, until the measured time raises the rate within a minute.

Once the sections are split over several primaries, how is the limit of six still kept?

A small fan_limits row, on one primary chosen by her fan id, keeps her entries: hold id, tickets and an expiry.

  1. A hold first adds its entry, with a guarded UPDATE that sums the entries that still count and refuses beyond six.
  2. It then claims the seats on its section's primary. If the claim fails, it removes the entry.
  3. PAYING moves the entry's expiry to pay_by, and the sale makes it permanent.

A crash between steps 1 and 2 can leave the two out of step. So the rule leans the safe way: an expired entry still counts until the payment checker has found its hold released on that section's primary. A lost write can then only lower her limit, never raise it.

Her one unpaid hold is kept the same way: a new hold releases the old one on its own primary before it replaces the old entry, so a lost release leaves the old entry counting.

After a 30-second failover, the 20,000 fans inside all retry at once. What stops them from knocking over the new primary?

The apps wait a random 1 to 5 seconds before they retry, so the 20,000 retries spread over about 4 seconds: about 5,000 requests a second, seven times the plan. Four limits keep that burst short. First, the door reopens at a quarter of its planned rate, 28 a second, so few new fans come in while the 20,000 already inside retry. Second, the booking servers keep fixed pools of database connections, a few dozen per primary in total across all servers. At about 700 transactions a second of a few milliseconds each, only about 5 are busy at once (Little's law again), so the pool absorbs a burst, and a request that finds it full gets 503 at once instead of waiting. Third, a lock_timeout of 1 second ends any claim that waits too long on a hot seat. Fourth, her own row keeps each fan to one hold in flight (its NOWAIT lock) and to 5 holds that commit in any 10 seconds. So the primary sees a bounded number of short transactions, and every fan above that hears "try again" within milliseconds.

A presale at 09:00 for a bank's card-holders, then the general on-sale at 10:00: how do they share one show?

As two lines in front of one set of seats. The doors table gains a line column in its primary key, so each line has its own doors row and its own door. Each line also has its own draw, because the line is added to the hash: HMAC(draw key, fan id, event id, line). The admissions table keeps one row per fan and event, and every hold still locks it, so the limit of six and her one unpaid hold span both lines. A fan whose presale pass has ended may join the 10:00 line like anyone else. When her turn in it comes, her admissions row is updated, as for any returning fan. Inside, both lines use the same holds and the same claim, so the two can never sell one seat twice. Whatever the presale does not sell is what the 10:00 line sells.
