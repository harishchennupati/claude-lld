  How to read this doc (30 min total)

  1. Pass 1 — headings only, 3 minutes. Scroll the whole page reading just the
     22 bold claim sentences. Don't read prose. This is the entire argument;
     everything else is evidence. If you can't hold 22 sentences, that's fine —
     you'll see them again.
  2. Pass 2 — full read, one direction, ~25 min. For each claim: read the
     heading, pause five seconds and guess why it's true, then read the prose to
     check your guess. The guess is the whole trick — it turns reading into
     retrieval. Wrong guesses are the most valuable moments; don't feel bad,
     mark them.
  3. Never re-read a paragraph on this pass. If it didn't land, put a mark and
     move on. Re-reading feels productive and mostly isn't; the next claim often
     explains the last one.
  4. At every red "The problem" box: stop, try to answer it yourself before 
     reading the next claim. That's the interview, in miniature, 9 times.
  5. Figures: read the prose first, then the figure, then the caption. The
     caption says the one thing the prose couldn't.

  Doubts — the rule

  Keep a scrap paper. Two columns: doubts and wrong guesses.

  - Doubt is a word you don't know (say "novation", "kernel-bypass"): stop and
    look it up, 30 seconds max. You cannot read past an unknown noun.
  - Doubt is "but why / what if": write it down, keep reading. Do not chase it.
    Most resolve within two claims; the rest are exactly what you bring to me
    afterwards. Chasing them mid-read is how a 25-minute read becomes a 3-hour

⏺ A lock is a "one at a time" gate around shared data: a thread must take the
  lock, change the book, release it. Contended means several threads want it at
  the same time — one gets in, the others wait in a queue. Two costs:

  - Time: taking a lock nobody else wants is ~20 ns. Taking one that others are
    fighting over is hundreds of nanoseconds — the CPU has to bounce the lock's
    memory between cores, and waiting threads may get parked and woken by the
    operating system.
  - Unpredictable: who wins the fight is decided by the CPU and the scheduler,
    not by you. So the same set of orders can be applied in a different sequence
    on two runs — and different sequences give different fills. That's the part
    that kills an exchange, because "same input, same output" is the whole
    audit story.

  In the naive design: many gateway threads all want to update the one AAPL book
  → they all fight over its lock → hundreds of nanoseconds per event out of a 2
  µs budget, and no two runs match. That's why step 1's answer is "one thread
  owns the book, no lock exists."
