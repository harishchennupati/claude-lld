The problem, as the interviewer puts it

“Design a payment gateway, the kind Razorpay or Stripe run. An online merchant plugs into you so that it can take card payments without ever dealing with a bank itself. At checkout, the merchant’s server calls your API to charge the buyer’s card. You pass the charge to a bank that connects to the card networks, the buyer types the OTP her own bank sends her, and then, while she waits, you get her bank’s yes or no within about a second. Fifty million payments a day. A merchant that retries a request must never charge a buyer twice. You keep the books: what was collected, what you owe each merchant and what you took in fees, and every morning your books must agree with the money the bank actually paid you. And you pay each merchant what it is owed, the next day.”

What this really is. We do not move money; the banks and the card network do that. A payment gateway is a bookkeeper with a telephone: it passes the merchant’s instructions to the banks and writes down what it believes happened to every rupee. An instruction can time out without telling us whether it ran, so the whole design is about knowing, for every rupee, what state it is in. In scope: card payments, from the merchant’s call to the merchant’s payout, and where it all runs. Out of scope: UPI, wallets, EMI, fraud scoring, foreign currency, a second region.

Start

Who is in the room, and what is asked

Who is who, and one payment from start to finish

Six roles, in the order a card payment travels. The design and every picture use the role names; the example next to each is only so the story has faces.

  1. The buyer, who pays. Example: Priya, buying shoes for ₹2,499 with her SBI Visa credit card.
  2. The merchant, who sells. Its server calls our API; it never talks to a bank and never sees the card number. Example: Myntra.
  3. Us, the gateway. We take the merchant's call, pass it to the acquirer, keep the books, and pay the merchant. The buyers' money reaches us in an escrow account: an account at our own bank, kept apart from our company's money, that holds what we owe merchants. Holding it makes us what the RBI calls a payment aggregator, which needs its licence. Example: Razorpay.
  4. The acquirer: a bank licensed to accept card payments on behalf of merchants, and the only door into the card network. We have contracts with two and call them over their APIs. Example: HDFC Bank, with Axis Bank as the second acquirer. On this page, the bank on its own means the acquirer; the others are named.
  5. The card network: the switch in the middle. It carries the question from the acquirer to the buyer's bank and the answer back in seconds, and the next day it moves the money between the two banks. Example: Visa.
  6. The issuer: the buyer's own bank, which issued her card. It checks her OTP, says yes or no, and is the bank that actually pays. Example: SBI.

Two clocks. A card payment runs on two clocks. The seconds clock runs while a person waits, in four steps: we create the payment; the buyer types her OTP; the issuer authorises, putting a hold on the money; and we capture, telling the acquirer to take the held money. The days clock runs while banks move money in nightly batches, where one bank transfer carries many payments: the acquirer settles, paying the money into our escrow account, and we pay out to the merchant. We answer the merchant on the seconds clock and pay it on the days clock, so what we believed in seconds has to be proven in days: that is why we keep books. The diagram follows one payment through all six steps, and its notes say what is true after each one.

The one-call mode. Some payments have no OTP: cards used outside India, and saved cards charged for a subscription. For them, create, authorise and capture are one API call: the merchant's server waits about a second and gets back captured. Nothing else on the page changes, and Part 2 draws this mode because it is easier to read. A refund travels the same path backwards and takes 5 to 7 days, because each bank on the way, the issuer last, posts it in its own cycle. A chargeback is different: the issuer pulls the money back weeks later because the buyer complained.

What the interviewer expects: five jobs and two numbers

  1. Take a payment and tell the merchant the outcome, with the issuer's answer within about a second of the OTP, because the buyer is watching a spinner.
  2. Never charge a buyer twice, however many times the merchant's server retries a request whose answer got lost.
  3. Refund, in full or in part, when the merchant asks.
  4. Tell the merchant what happened: an API it can ask, and webhooks, where we call the merchant's server when something changes so it does not have to keep asking.
  5. Pay the merchant what it is owed, keep our fee, and keep books that balance and match what the acquirer actually paid us.

It must survive a server of ours dying mid-payment, a bank that answers slowly or not at all, a whole data centre going dark, and a merchant retrying wildly. It must answer every API call fast. It must be available 99.99% of the time, because when we are down, every merchant's checkout is down. It must never lose or invent a single rupee. And it must keep card numbers off our ordinary servers, as PCI DSS, the card industry's security standard, requires.

Two numbers, then the rest by proportion. Take 50 million payments a day, a large Indian gateway.

At 3,000 payments a second, with each one keeping a server of ours waiting on the bank for about a second, about 3,000 payments are in progress at any moment; a few dozen stateless servers carry that. Each payment makes a few small database commits, about 12,000 a second in all, so eight Postgres shards take roughly 1,500 each, which is comfortable. Each payment also produces about five events, 15,000 Kafka messages a second at the peak, which one small cluster handles easily. The number to write on the board is 3,000.

The design

The whole design in one picture

How to derive this design yourselfthe method

You don't need to remember this design. Split the requirements in two. The functional ones say what the system must do: for each, start from the first idea anyone would have, see what goes wrong, and fix that. The non-functional ones say what it must survive: run the design so far against each, and add what it lacks. Twelve rows rebuild the whole picture.

Functional: what it must do

what it must dothe first ideawhat goes wrongwhat we do instead
F1. Take a payment, while the buyer types her OTPThe merchant calls us, we call the acquirer, and we reply with the result.The issuer waits for the buyer's OTP, up to a minute, and the merchant's call times out long before.Reply pending at once, and send the result later by webhook; the merchant can also ask with GET.
Webhook and GET, not just one. Polling alone makes every merchant ask every few seconds; webhooks alone leave a merchant stuck when one is lost.
F2. Never charge twice, however often the merchant retriesBefore charging, check whether this order is already paid.A lost reply makes the merchant retry, and two retries a moment apart both see not paid and both charge. A broken merchant can also retry in a tight loop.The merchant sends an idempotency key, an id it makes up for this charge and repeats unchanged on every retry. Before we call the acquirer, we insert the key under a unique index; a retry's insert fails, so the retry gets the first request's stored answer instead of charging again. An API gateway in front limits how fast each merchant may call, so a retry loop can't flood us.
Postgres, not Redis. Redis is faster, but a failover can drop its last writes, and a dropped key is a double charge. In the payments database the key sits beside the payment and survives whatever the payment survives.
F3. Capture and refund safely, in full or in partRead the payment's status in code, check it, then update the row.Two requests read AUTHORIZED at the same moment and both capture; two partial refunds each pass the check and together exceed the charge.Let the database check, inside the update: WHERE status = 'AUTHORIZED', or WHERE refunded + 50000 <= captured. Of two racing updates only one matches, and only the winner then calls the acquirer.
Not a lock. A lock in our code works only inside one server, and a lock in Redis can vanish in a failover; the database already locks the row it updates, for free.
F4. Hear back from the bank: refunds finishing, chargebacksThe acquirer calls our webhook, and we do all the work inside that call.If we are slow, the acquirer times out and retries, harder and harder, and a slow minute becomes a flood.An inbound receiver saves each call to an inbox table and replies 200 at once; a worker applies it later. The acquirer's event id makes a resend harmless.
A table, not a queue. A queue would absorb the bank's calls just as well, but in our own database, applying an event and marking it done are one transaction, so a crash can't apply it twice.
F5. Tell the merchant what happenedWhen a payment changes, our server calls the merchant's webhook URL right there, in the same request.If our server crashes after saving the change but before the call, the merchant never hears. And if the merchant's server is slow, our request waits for it.A transactional outbox: the news is saved as a row in the same transaction as the change, so it can't be lost. After the commit, a relay publishes new outbox rows to Kafka, and a webhook sender POSTs each one to the merchant, retrying for 24 hours.
An outbox, not change data capture. Reading changes from the database log (Debezium) saves the extra table, but it publishes raw row changes instead of events we designed, and ties every reader to our table layout. Postgres and Kafka cannot share one transaction, so an outbox table is the simplest way to save a change and its news together. Many teams do run Debezium, but point it at the outbox table: the same idea, with the database log read instead of the table polled.
F6. Feed our own dashboardsLet the dashboards run their queries on the payments database.Dashboards sum months of payments, and those big reads slow down the database that is taking payments right now.A reporting consumer reads the same Kafka events as the webhook sender, as its own consumer group and at its own pace, and copies them into ClickHouse, skipping any event it has already seen. The payout needs no copy: it runs on each shard at night and reads the ledger itself, because it must stamp the entries it pays in the same transaction.
Kafka, not RabbitMQ or SQS. SQS is the easiest to run and RabbitMQ routes messages flexibly, but a queue deletes a message once a reader has handled it: two readers need two copies, and nothing can be replayed after a bug. Kafka keeps every event for days, each reader keeps its own place, a new reader (fraud checks, say) needs no change to the payment path, and the payment id as key keeps one payment's events in order. It costs a cluster to run.
ClickHouse, not a Postgres replica. A replica still reads row by row; a column store sums months of payments in seconds.
F7. Keep books that balance: where every rupee is, and whoseA balance column per merchant, increased by its share on each capture.A balance says how much, not where the money sits or whose it is, so it can't tell ₹2,499 still owed by the acquirer from ₹2,499 already in our escrow account. And a crash between the payment and the balance, or a bug in the amount, leaves it wrong with nothing to notice.A double-entry ledger. Every movement of money is written as entries on two kinds of account. One kind says where the money is: acquirer owes us, our escrow account. The other says whose it is: the merchant's, our fee, the GST. The two kinds always total the same: capturing ₹2,499 is +2,499 on acquirer owes us, against +2,440 for the merchant, +50 fee and +9 GST, so a mistake shows as totals that stop matching. The entries commit in the same transaction as the payment's new state, which needs transactions across rows: that is why the database is Postgres.
Postgres, not Cassandra or DynamoDB. Those grow across machines by themselves, but Cassandra has no ACID transactions across rows with different partition keys, DynamoDB's are capped at 100 items and cost double, and neither can do the nightly match as one SQL join. What Postgres costs us is sharding it ourselves (N4); MySQL would do just as well.
F8. Match the bank, and pay the merchant the next dayTrust our own books, and each night have a cron job add up yesterday's captures and send each merchant one transfer.Money moves at the banks without any event reaching us: a refund typed into the acquirer's portal, a settlement paid short. Adding up captures ignores refunds and chargebacks. A transfer retried after a timeout pays the merchant twice. And cron can run a job twice, or not at all.Every night, reconciliation matches the acquirer's settlement file against our payments by our reference, and each mismatch, which accountants call a break, goes on a breaks list for a person. Then a payout job pays each merchant's available balance, the money that has landed in our escrow account, with our own reference on the transfer so that our bank refuses a repeat. A job scheduler runs these jobs once each, in order.
Airflow, not cron. Cron is simpler, but it can run a job twice on two servers, or not at all on a dead one, and it records nothing. Airflow retries, runs jobs in order and records every run; it costs one more system to operate.

N4. 3,000 payments a second, 36 TB a year, 99.99%, even if a zone goes darkOne Postgres server, with a copy that follows a moment behind.About 12,000 writes a second and 36 TB a year strain one machine, and at that size rebuilding a lost copy or restoring a backup takes a day. Each shard has a synchronous standby in a second zone, a separate data centre in the same city with its own power and network: a commit is confirmed only once the standby has it.
By merchant, not by payment id or a hash ring. Payment ids spread the load perfectly but scatter each merchant's ledger over all 8 shards. A hash ring (consistent hashing) moves many merchants whenever a shard is added; a directory, a small table saying which shard each merchant lives on, moves one merchant at a time. The cost: a giant merchant can crowd its shard, so it gets moved to a quieter one.
Synchronous, not asynchronous or a second active region. Asynchronous copying makes every commit faster but loses the last ones in a failover. Two active regions survive a whole region going dark, but every commit would wait tens of milliseconds for the other region. A standby in the next zone adds about a millisecond.

The API: what a merchant can call

A merchant has nine calls, all in the fold below. Three rules hold for all of them: the merchant authenticates with a secret API key in the Authorization header (a separate test key routes to a fake bank); every POST that creates or moves money carries an Idempotency-Key header; every amount is an integer in paisa. The one call that matters, worked:

  "payment": { "id": "pay_1", "status": "captured", "captured": 249900, "our_ref": "T1" } }

The redirect is our own 3-D Secure page: it starts the OTP check with the issuer and sends the buyer on to the issuer's page. A webhook carries an event id, the type, the time, and the payment as GET would have returned it at that moment. It is a snapshot, and a retry can deliver it hours late, after a refund or a chargeback has changed the payment, so before the merchant acts on money, it calls GET, which always returns the current state.

The merchant sees a simpler status than our states. pending covers CREATED and UNKNOWN, and also AUTHORIZED under auto capture, where we capture as soon as the issuer approves. Under manual capture, where the merchant captures later with its own call, an approved hold shows as authorized. Every other state shows under its own name: captured, settled, voided, failed, refunded.

If the interviewer pushes

  • What errors can a merchant get? Few, and each says what to do. 401: the API key is wrong. 402: the issuer declined; the answer carries its reason code and a message that is safe to show the buyer. 409: the same idempotency key is still in progress, or the payment is not in a state that allows this move, such as a second capture; read GET. 422: the same idempotency key came with a different body, or a refund is larger than what is left to refund. 429: slow down.
  • How do you change the API without breaking merchants? Versions are pinned per merchant (API-Version: 2026-01), so a field can be renamed without breaking an old integration.

How it is split. The payments database is sharded by merchant. The four questions an interviewer asks about that:

Why by merchant, not by payment id?So one payment's row, key, entries and outbox row live on one shard, and the final commit is one local transaction. By payment id, a merchant's ledger would be spread across every shard, and every balance and every nightly match would be a cross-shard job. Part 2

The six hard parts

Never charge twice: the idempotency key

The problem. Take the one-call mode, where one call does everything. At 19:04:02 the merchant's server posts the ₹2,499 charge; we authorise and capture it; on the way back the answer dies because a load balancer restarted. The merchant's client gives up after five seconds and sends the same request again, and nothing in it looks different from the first. Treat it as new and the buyer pays ₹4,998.

The fix is the idempotency key the API requires on every POST that moves money. The merchant makes it up once per thing it wants to charge (its order id plus an attempt number, so a second try after a decline is a new key) and sends it unchanged on every retry. We keep it for seven days, as a row in idempotency_keys whose primary key is (merchant, endpoint, key). A primary key is a unique index, so the database itself refuses the second insert and decides which request owns the key. There is no gap between checking for the key and inserting it, and no lock in our own code, even when two servers try in the same millisecond.

INSERT INTO idempotency_keys (merchant_id, endpoint, key, status, request_hash)
VALUES ('mer_42', 'POST /payments', 'ord-771-1', 'IN_PROGRESS', sha256(body));
-- ERROR: duplicate key -> somebody owns this key already: read the row, answer from it

The one hole. The server that wins the key can die before it answers.

If the interviewer pushes

  • Why is the key scoped to (merchant, endpoint, key) and not just the key? Two merchants can both use ord-771 as an order id, and one merchant can send the same string to create a payment and later to refund it; scoping keeps one call from being mistaken for another.
  • How long do you keep a key, and what happens after that? Seven days, which outlasts any sane retry loop. The table is partitioned by day, and a daily task drops the partition that is eight days old, which is instant. A retry with an older key is treated as a new payment, so the API reference states the window.

The payment's states, and updates that refuse to go backwards

The problem. A merchant captures the same order twice by accident. Another refunds ₹3,000 against a ₹2,499 charge. The acquirer's settlement row arrives for a payment voided an hour ago. If each case is handled by its own if statement somewhere in the code, each is a separate chance for a bug, and the second and third would send out money we never took in. The fix: name the states, allow only certain moves between them, and let the database enforce each move with one UPDATE that names the state it is leaving.

UPDATE payments SET status = 'CAPTURED', captured = 249900
WHERE id = 'pay_1' AND status = 'AUTHORIZED';
-- 1 row: ours, so now tell the acquirer.
-- 0 rows: already captured, voided or failed: roll back, answer 409

UPDATE payments SET refunded = refunded + 50000
WHERE id = 'pay_1' AND refunded + disputed + 50000 <= captured;
-- ₹500 on ₹2,499: 1 row.  ₹3,000: 0 rows, refused.
-- A chargeback raises disputed, so no refund can repeat it.

Why the database, not our code. Two servers capture the same payment at the same instant.

If the interviewer pushes

  • The acquirer's webhook and the sweeper learn the answer at the same moment. Who applies it? Both try the same guarded UPDATE out of UNKNOWN. Whichever commits first moves the row and writes the entries and the outbox row; the other changes no rows and stops, so nothing is written twice.
  • What happens to a payment the merchant authorises but never captures? The issuer drops an unused hold by itself after about a week. Before that, the sweeper voids holds older than five days (the last query in the picture above): the row moves to VOIDED through the usual guarded UPDATE, the hold is released, and the merchant gets a payment.voided webhook.

The ledger: how the money is written down

The problem. The ₹2,499 changes hands three times over two days, from the issuer to the acquirer to our escrow account to the merchant, and ₹59 of it stays with us and the government. At any moment we must be able to say where each rupee is and whose it is.

The fix is double-entry bookkeeping. An account is a bucket of money we track, and buckets come in two kinds: where the money is (acquirer owes us, our escrow account) and whose it is (the merchant's share, our fee, the government's GST). Every movement is written as entries, and after every movement the two kinds total the same, because every rupee that is somewhere belongs to someone. The merchant's share sits in two accounts: pending until its money lands in escrow, then available, and only available money is paid out.

The capture's four entries (the first moment in the picture) commit in the same transaction as the payment row and the outbox row. So nothing we do can move money without our books knowing; money the banks move without telling us is for reconciliation to catch. Four smaller rules complete the ledger:

If the interviewer pushes

  • Every payment writes three company-wide rows: fee income, GST payable and acquirer owes us. Are they hot rows, rows so many transactions update that they queue for the lock? Yes: each is hit about 375 times a second on a shard at the peak, and every update holds the row's lock until its commit has reached the standby. They are read rarely, so each is split into sixteen sub-rows (fee_income_0 to fee_income_15); an entry adds to a random one, and a read adds the sixteen together. A merchant's own accounts stay single rows until the merchant becomes a giant, and then they are split the same way.
  • A big chargeback leaves a merchant owing us money. What then? Its available balance goes negative, so the payout pays nothing and tomorrow's sales repay the debt first. This is what the reserve is for: the part of each payout held back from risky merchants (see the payout section). If the debt is larger than the reserve, we invoice the merchant.

If the interviewer pushes

  • Why is the payment id the message key, and not the merchant id? The key only has to keep one payment's events in order, and the payment id does that. Keyed by merchant, a giant merchant's whole traffic would land on one partition and one worker, which would become the bottleneck.
  • How big does the outbox get, and how do you keep it small? About 250 million rows a day across the shards. Each shard's outbox table is split into one sub-table per day (Postgres table partitioning), and a day's sub-table is dropped once all its rows are published. Dropping a sub-table is instant, while deleting rows one by one would bloat the table.

Proving the books: the day channel, then the night

The problem. Everything so far is what we believe happened. The banks keep their own record, and it can hold events we never saw: a chargeback, a refund someone typed straight into the acquirer's portal during an outage, or a settlement paid one transaction short. Their version reaches us through two channels: the acquirer's webhooks during the day, and two files at night, the acquirer's settlement file and our bank's statement.

The day channel is an inbox table, bank_events.

The night is six tasks run by the job scheduler. A task that works on the payments database runs once for each shard, and the whole run must finish before the next day's file arrives.

  • How long does the night take, and what if the file is late? Loading and match 1 run per shard in parallel, about an hour for 25 million rows on eight shards; doubling the payments doubles the shards, not the hours. If the file is late, that day's payout waits for it.
  • Why not reconcile continuously instead of once a night? The acquirer's file is the only complete record, and it arrives once a day. The day channel already applies what the bank tells us as it happens; the night proves that nothing was missed.

Paying the merchant, and taking money back

The problem. Each merchant must be paid once a day, and only for money that has landed in our escrow account, while what it is owed keeps changing as refunds and chargebacks come off. The diagram is the sixth task of the night.

WITH paid AS (
  UPDATE ledger_entries SET payout_id = 'po_1'
  WHERE account_id = 'mer_42:available' AND payout_id IS NULL
  RETURNING amount)
INSERT INTO payouts (id, merchant_id, amount, status, our_ref)
SELECT 'po_1', 'mer_42', SUM(amount), 'SENT', 'N123' FROM paid;

RETURNING hands back exactly the rows the UPDATE stamped, and the WITH lets the INSERT sum those same rows, so the stamp and the sum cannot disagree: an entry committed a moment later waits for tomorrow, and a second run finds nothing left to stamp. If the sum is zero or less, the transaction rolls back and nothing is paid.

The transfer is bank to bank, from our escrow account to the merchant's account, over the RBI's transfer systems: NEFT for most payouts, RTGS for ₹2 lakh and above (its minimum), and IMPS for merchants on an instant plan. The card network is not involved; it only moves money between the issuer and the acquirer.

For merchants whose chargebacks run above 1%, we hold back 5 to 10% of each payout as a reserve for 120 days, the window in which almost all chargebacks arrive; the reserve is an entry that moves that part out of the available account. Our own ₹59 a payment leaves escrow once a month as our commission, which the RBI's escrow rules allow as long as escrow always holds what merchants are owed; the ₹9 of GST in it goes on to the government with the monthly return.

If the interviewer pushes

  • Two schedulers both think they should run the payout. What stops a double payout? The scheduler and the payout job's lock row per shard make a second run rare, but a process that freezes can wake up after its lock has expired, so neither is enough alone. What makes a second run harmless is the query under the payout diagram: it stamps and sums the same rows in one statement, so a second run finds nothing left to pay. And a transfer retried after a timeout carries the same reference, N123, which our bank refuses twice.
  • How would you pay a merchant the same day? Pay from our own money before the acquirer settles, for a fee, and book it in the ledger as an advance that the next settlement repays. The risk becomes ours: a chargeback or a short settlement now has to be recovered from the merchant.

Part 3

Running it

Where it runs: one region, three zones

A region is one city where the cloud provider has data centres, Mumbai for us; an availability zone is one of those buildings, with its own power and network, a millisecond from the others. We run in one region across three zones: whatever decides money keeps a synchronous copy in a second zone, whatever holds no state runs in all three, and whatever can be rebuilt is copied loosely.

If the interviewer pushes

  • Why three zones and not two? With two, when they lose contact neither can tell whether the other is dead or only cut off, so neither may promote a standby. The third zone breaks the tie: only the side that still sees two of the three may take writes.
  • Why not a second region as well? A synchronous copy hundreds of kilometres away adds tens of milliseconds to every commit, and an asynchronous one can lose the last payments. The failure table below prices losing the region instead.

Consistent or available: the CAP choice

Sometimes the network splits and two groups of machines cannot reach each other: a network partition. During one, each part of our system must choose between consistency, where every read sees the one latest truth, and availability, where every request gets an answer, perhaps a stale one. That choice is the CAP theorem, and each part of the design makes it by what a wrong answer would cost.

Here is what that choice looks like when a zone is cut off but keeps running. It rests on a lease: the primary may take writes only while it holds one, for 30 seconds at a time, and it must keep renewing it with the cluster manager (Patroni, for example), the software that watches every primary and promotes a standby when one dies.

Even without a partition, consistency costs time: each commit waits about a millisecond for the standby. Across regions the wait would be tens of milliseconds, so the money path stays in one region. Interviewers call this second trade-off PACELC: if there is a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency; Else, when the network is fine, choose Latency or Consistency.

If the interviewer pushes

  • Why not choose availability and fix conflicts later, like a shopping cart? Two carts merge by keeping both items. Two versions of a payment, one captured and one refunded, need a person to untangle, so refusing writes for 30 seconds is cheaper.
  • What does the merchant see in those 30 seconds? A 503 with a Retry-After header. Its retry carries the same idempotency key, so it cannot charge twice.

The technical round: nine questions interviewers push onthe last round
Why not a two-phase commit across Postgres and Kafka, or a saga?

The goal is that a change and its news are saved together, or not at all. A two-phase commit would make both systems agree first and then both save. But Kafka cannot take part in one, and even if it could, every payment would wait on both systems and stop whenever either was down. The outbox gets the same result with one ordinary commit: the news is just another Postgres row, and the relay sends it to Kafka afterwards. A saga, a chain of steps where each step has an undo, is only needed when one change spans several systems; our one such flow is with the acquirer, where a void undoes an authorisation.

What isolation level do the guarded UPDATEs need?

The default one, READ COMMITTED, is enough. When two captures reach the same row, Postgres makes the second one wait for the first to finish, then checks the second one's WHERE again against the updated row. The row now says CAPTURED, so the second capture changes nothing. The strictest level, SERIALIZABLE, would also be correct, but it cancels more transactions when the system is busy, and we do not need it, because each rule sits in one row's WHERE.

Why does the idempotency key commit on its own, before the bank call, and not in the final transaction?

So that it survives our server dying mid-call. In the one-call mode, POST /payments waits about a second for the acquirer. If the key sat in one long transaction with everything else, a crash in that second would erase the key while the charge stood at the bank, and the retry would charge again; and every payment in flight would hold a database connection open for that second. Committed first, as IN_PROGRESS, the key is already there: a retry gets a 409, and the sweeper settles whatever the dead server left. When the acquirer answers, the final transaction marks the key DONE and stores the answer, together with the new state, the entries and the outbox row.

Kafka is down for an hour. What happens?

Payments carry on, because a payment request never talks to Kafka: the news waits as outbox rows in Postgres, about 10 million of them an hour, which Postgres holds easily. When Kafka is back, the relay sends the backlog in order, so webhooks and dashboards arrive an hour late, but nothing is missing. The alarm that fires is the age of the oldest unsent outbox row.

How do you keep one payment's events in order, all the way to the merchant?

Three things keep them in order. Outbox rows get an increasing id on each shard, and the relay sends them in that order. The Kafka key is the payment id, so all of one payment's events go to the same partition, a slice of the topic that a reader reads in order. Only a webhook retry can still deliver an older event late, so each event says when it happened, and the merchant checks GET for the current state.

Is exactly-once delivery possible, and where does each reader remember what it has seen?

Not between two systems: when an acknowledgement is lost, the sender cannot tell whether its message arrived, so it has to send again. So we deliver at least once, and every reader skips repeats in its own way. The webhook sender records (endpoint, event id) under a unique index, in the same transaction as the delivery row, so the record and the work happen together. Reporting's ClickHouse table merges repeats of an event id away in the background, and dashboard queries ask for the merged result.

After a capture, can the merchant read its own write at once?

Yes. GET reads the shard's primary, the database where the commit happened, so it always sees it. Only the nightly check reads the copy that runs about a second behind, where a little staleness is fine. Even after a failover, the new primary already holds every confirmed commit, so the merchant still sees its own writes.

Your shard key is the merchant. How do you answer a question across merchants, such as every payment made with one card?

Not in Postgres: a question across merchants would have to visit every shard, and Postgres is split so that each query touches one merchant. Every event already flows into ClickHouse, which is built for questions that scan millions of rows, so questions across merchants are answered there, a few seconds behind. For one card, tokens do not help, because each checkout gets a new one; so each payment also stores the network's card reference (PAR), which names the card without being card data.

What is the latency budget for POST /payments?

About 40 ms to reply pending. That covers the API gateway's checks of the API key and the rate limit, saving the idempotency key and the payment row on the merchant's shard, and one commit that waits about a millisecond for the standby. The issuer's second and the buyer's minute on the OTP page both happen outside this request, which is exactly why we reply pending instead of waiting.
